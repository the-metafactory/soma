# R2 — Bitter Lesson vs Scaffolding: The Evidence in Agent Engineering

## TLDR

The evidence does not support either pole. It supports a **layered answer**: scaffolding that *substitutes for model capability* (hand-coded reasoning steps, prompt chains, rigid multi-agent choreography, compensatory "crutches") has repeatedly been deleted or made obsolete by model upgrades — this is the Bitter Lesson operating at the application layer, and practitioners from Anthropic's Claude Code team to Manus (re-architected five times in ~18 months) confirm it. But scaffolding that *defines and verifies outcomes* — typed state, deterministic gates, evals, environment/tool plumbing, cost control, audit trails — is precisely what the strongest empirical results say you need: tau-bench shows the best agents' pass^8 collapses below 25% despite >60% single-run success; METR shows the 80%-reliability horizon is roughly 5× shorter than the 50% horizon; Princeton's "AI Agents That Matter" shows simple deterministic baselines beat complex agents at ~50× lower cost; and Agentless — a fixed three-stage deterministic pipeline — beat every open-source autonomous agent of its era and was adopted by OpenAI for o3's SWE-bench numbers. The framework-churn era (LangChain exodus, 2023–24) shows the failure mode is not scaffolding per se but *abstraction over a moving target*. The practitioner consensus that emerges: **own thin, deterministic control flow and durable verification; rent (and expect to delete) anything that compensates for model weakness.** For Soma — whose bet is deterministic TypeScript contracts over prompt conventions — the evidence says the bet is sound *if* the contracts sit at the verification/state/gate layer, and unsound if they encode reasoning procedure.

## 1. The pro-freedom canon: Sutton, Karpathy-era commentary, and "the model deletes your code"

**Sutton's original claim.** [The Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html) (Rich Sutton, 2019) argues from 70 years of AI history that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin," and that building in human knowledge "helps in the short term… but plateaus and even inhibits progress in the long run" (chess, Go, speech, vision are his case studies). Note what the essay actually targets: *hand-encoded knowledge about how to think*, not infrastructure. Sutton says nothing against environments, evaluation, or plumbing — search and learning, his two winning "general methods," both presuppose an environment and a signal. Sutton himself has since pushed the point further against LLMs ([Dwarkesh interview, 2025](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/richard-sutton)), arguing they lack ground-truth learning from experience — an argument that, ironically, *raises* the value of external verification loops.

**The application-layer restatement.** The modern practitioner form is: as models improve, harness structure must be *stripped away*. Hugo Bowne-Anderson's writeup of agent-harness practice ([AI Agent Harness, 3 Principles, and the Bitter Lesson Revisited](https://hugobowne.substack.com/p/ai-agent-harness-3-principles-for)) quotes the principle directly: "Over time models get better and you're having to strip away structure, remove assumptions and make your harness or your system simpler and adapt to the models." Its evidence: **Manus re-architected five times since March 2024; LangChain's Open Deep Research rebuilt multiple times within a year; Claude Code's harness continually stripped down** as models advanced. Compensatory control logic gets deleted; tool execution, message history, and context management survive (but get simpler). The same framing appears in [lowtouch.ai's "Rethinking AI Agent Scaffolding"](https://www.lowtouch.ai/rethinking-ai-agent-scaffolding-embracing-the-bitter-lesson-for-scalable-automation/) and in [Honeycomb's "If it Wanted to, it Would"](https://www.honeycomb.io/blog/if-it-wanted-to-it-would-bitter-lesson-llm-users): minimal engineering + maximal compute scales with model improvements; clever engineering around model deficits does not.

**Karpathy's actual position is more nuanced than the meme.** In the [Dwarkesh interview (Oct 2025)](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/andrej-karpathy) he argues this is "the decade of agents, not the year of agents" — current models "are cognitively lacking… it will take about a decade to work through all of those issues" ([HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45619329), [The Neuron summary](https://www.theneuron.ai/explainer-articles/andrej-karpathy-told-dwarkesh-that-agi-is-still-a-decade-away)). Two things follow, and both matter: (a) because models are deficient *now*, scaffolding — memory systems, context engineering ("LLM-as-CPU, context-window-as-RAM"), structured loops — is *currently necessary*; (b) because they will improve, any given piece of compensatory scaffolding has a shelf life. Karpathy is simultaneously the source of "agents don't work yet, you need structure" and of the expectation that the structure is temporary. His CLAUDE.md-style guideline experiments point the same way: rules that *constrain* the agent (don't add unasked features, don't "improve" nearby code) measurably reduced tool calls, time, and cost ([Augment Code analysis](https://www.augmentcode.com/blog/karpathy-skills-on-openclaw-agents-don-t-write-better-code-but-they-do-it-more-efficiently), [jonbeckett.com](https://jonbeckett.com/2026/05/19/karpathy-guidelines-taming-ai-coding-agents/)) — that is *governance* scaffolding, and it paid off.

**The startup version.** Sam Altman's "we will steamroll you" warning to GPT-wrapper startups ([techstartups.com](https://techstartups.com/2024/04/22/sam-altman-openai-is-going-to-steamroll-you-if-your-startup-is-a-wrapper-on-gpt-4/), [the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/sam-altman-explains-why-openai-might-steamroll-your-ai-startup/)): of two strategies — build assuming models stagnate vs. assuming they keep improving — "95% of the world should bet on the latter." The corollary practitioners draw ([Allen Pike, "Pushing the Frontier"](https://allenpike.com/2024/pushing-the-frontier/)) is to build things that get *better* when the model improves, and defensibility via data, workflow integration, and evaluation — not via cleverness the next model absorbs.

## 2. Anthropic's "Building Effective Agents": the canonical middle position

[Anthropic's guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) (Dec 2024) is routinely cited by both camps, which tells you it is actually a synthesis:

- "The most successful implementations weren't using complex frameworks or specialized libraries. Instead, they were building with **simple, composable patterns**." (anti-framework — pro-freedom in the LangChain sense)
- It distinguishes **workflows** (LLMs orchestrated through *predefined code paths*) from **agents** (LLM directs its own tool use in a loop), and says: use the simplest pattern that passes evaluation — often a workflow or a single well-tooled LLM call; reserve agents for tasks where you cannot hardcode the path *but can still verify progress*. (pro-deterministic-scaffold)
- Agents "trade predictability for flexibility"; each autonomous turn adds latency, cost, and error-propagation risk — so agentic autonomy must be paid for with **guardrails and verification** ([pattern analysis at agentpatterns.ai](https://www.agentpatterns.ai/agent-design/anthropic-effective-agents-framework/), [mer.vin summary](https://mer.vin/2026/05/when-not-to-build-ai-agents-anthropics-workflow-vs-agent-playbook/)).

Anthropic's own SWE-bench work is the cleanest demonstration of *which* scaffolding matters: their [SWE-bench Verified post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/swe-bench-sonnet) reached 49% (then-SOTA) with a deliberately **minimal scaffold** — a prompt, a bash tool, an edit tool — on the philosophy of "give as much control as possible to the model itself." The scaffold they kept was the *environment* (tools, execution, test harness); everything procedural was deleted.

## 3. The counter-evidence: where deterministic structure measurably wins

**Reliability collapses without gates (tau-bench).** [τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) (Sierra, 2024) introduced **pass^k** — probability that *all* k runs succeed. The best agent (GPT-4o function calling) had >60% average success but **pass^8 < 25%** in retail, worse in airline. Free-running agents are not merely imperfect; they are *inconsistent* — the same task fails on rerun. The authors explicitly call for "methods that improve the ability of agents to act consistently and reliably follow rules." This is the strongest single datapoint for typed state and policy gates: rule-following is exactly what deterministic code does with probability 1.

**The reliability gap on long horizons (METR).** [METR's "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks"](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/) ([arXiv 2503.14499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)) found the 50%-success time horizon doubling every ~7 months (GPT-2 ≈ 2s → Claude 3.7 ≈ 50 min → o3 ≈ 2h; by 2026 frontier 50%-horizons are reported around 12h). Two pro-scaffold facts hide inside the pro-freedom trendline: (1) the **80%-reliability horizon is several-fold (~5×) shorter** than the 50% horizon — autonomy at production-grade reliability lags autonomy at coin-flip reliability by years; (2) failures on long tasks come less from missing skills than from "struggling to string together longer sequences of actions" — i.e., error recovery and state discipline, the things external checkpoints and gates supply. Simultaneously, the doubling trend is the strongest quantitative argument that *compensatory* scaffolding has a hard expiry date.

**Simple deterministic pipelines beat autonomous agents — twice over (Agentless, Princeton).** [Agentless](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01489) (2024): a fixed three-stage pipeline (hierarchical localization → parallel patch generation → test-based patch selection), no autonomous decisions, no complex tools — achieved the **highest score among all open-source systems on SWE-bench Lite (27.33%) at the lowest cost ($0.34/issue)**, and was later [adopted by OpenAI as the harness for o3's SWE-bench results](https://x.com/_philschmid/status/1878395725404344390). Note the twist: Agentless is *anti-agent* but its winning ingredient is deterministic structure plus **test-based verification** for patch selection. Princeton's [AI Agents That Matter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502) (Kapoor, Stroebl, Siegel, Nadgir, Narayanan) found pervasive evaluation malpractice and showed **simple baselines (retry, warming, escalation) match or beat SOTA complex agents on HumanEval at ~50× lower cost**; they argue evaluations must be jointly cost-and-accuracy controlled — cost control being itself a deterministic-harness function.

**The harness, not the model, drives quality variance.** ["Don't Blame the Large Language Model"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03691) (2026) held the LLM fixed across 35 sequential releases of an agent CLI and traced quality regressions to harness changes — context management, tool execution, architectural decisions in individual PRs. Practitioners misattribute regressions to the model; the scaffold is empirically load-bearing. Meanwhile [Dissecting the SWE-Bench Leaderboards](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17208v1) finds gains from *increasing scaffolding complexity* saturating, with performance increasingly determined by the underlying model. Read together: harness *quality* matters enormously; harness *complexity* buys nothing.

**Auditability and interruption.** [HumanLayer's 12-Factor Agents](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/main/content/factor-08-own-your-control-flow.md) distills production experience: successful LLM products are "mostly deterministic code" with LLM decision points; agents should be **stateless reducers over an owned event log**; "own your control flow" so you can pause between tool *selection* and tool *invocation* (the human-approval gate), resume, and audit ([overview](https://www.humanlayer.dev/blog/12-factor-agents), [analysis](https://starlog.is/articles/ai-agents/humanlayer-12-factor-agents/)). None of that is possible in a framework-hidden while-loop.

## 4. The framework post-mortems: what actually failed in the LangChain era

[Octomind's "Why we no longer use LangChain"](https://octomind.dev/blog/why-we-no-longer-use-langchain-for-building-our-ai-agents) (12 months in production, 2023–24) and the accompanying [HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40739982) are the canonical post-mortem: too many abstraction layers, APIs breaking every release, "huge stack traces and debugging framework code instead of application logic." Their diagnosis is the important part: **"the building blocks LangChain abstracted were not static — it's impossible to elegantly abstract something this dynamic"**; abstractions are safe only for lower-level, stable building blocks ([further criticism roundup](https://shashankguda.medium.com/challenges-criticisms-of-langchain-b26afcef94e7)). This is *not* evidence against deterministic scaffolding — Octomind replaced LangChain with their own modular deterministic code, not with a free agent. The failure class is **third-party abstraction over a fast-moving layer**, i.e., renting opinions about how the model should think. Framework churn since (LangChain→LangGraph rewrites, CrewAI/AutoGen pivots, Manus ×5) confirms the pattern: the layer that keeps getting rewritten is orchestration-of-reasoning; the layers that persist are tools, data, and evals.

## 5. What survives model upgrades — the future-proofing consensus

Across every source above, the same partition emerges:

**Survives (appreciates with better models):**
- **Verification & evals** — test-based selection (Agentless), pass^k-style reliability measurement (tau-bench), outcome evals that "survive prompt rewrites and model upgrades because they encode what the product is for" ([amux Harness Engineering guide](https://amux.io/guides/harness-engineering/), [Arize on Anthropic-style evals](https://arize.com/blog/ai-agent-evaluation-how-to-build-evals-you-can-trust/)). Shunyu Yao's [The Second Half](https://ysymyth.github.io/The-Second-Half/) generalizes it: the field's second half is task definition, environment design, and reward structure — "evaluation becomes more important than training."
- **Environment & tools** — bash + edit + tests was Anthropic's entire winning scaffold; tool quality and execution plumbing carried harness quality in the fixed-model study ([2607.03691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03691)).
- **Owned control flow, state, and gates** — event logs, pause/resume, human-approval points, typed state (12-Factor Agents); these are audit and safety properties no model upgrade provides.
- **Data plumbing & context management** — Karpathy's "context engineering as OS problem"; retained-but-simplified in every harness account.

**Gets deleted (depreciates with better models):**
- Hand-coded reasoning procedures, prompt chains encoding *how* to think (Sutton's exact target).
- Compensatory crutches for model weaknesses — parsing fixups, elaborate role choreography, forced decomposition (Manus/Claude Code/Open Deep Research strip-downs).
- Third-party abstraction frameworks over the moving layer (LangChain exodus).
- Complexity-for-complexity: scaffolding elaboration past saturation ([2506.17208](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17208v1); simple baselines at 50× lower cost, [2407.01502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502)).

**Implication for Soma.** Deterministic TypeScript contracts at the *checkpoint/verification/state* layer (criterion + evidence + verdict + gate) sit squarely in the "survives" column — they are what tau-bench and METR say free agents lack, and what Yao says the field will increasingly be made of. The Bitter Lesson risk is confined to any Soma component that scripts the model's reasoning path; the mitigation is Anthropic's rule — use the simplest pattern that passes evaluation, and let the eval, not the orchestration, be the permanent asset.

## Verification appendix

*Method: fetched and checked the 10 most load-bearing sources directly (arXiv abstracts/HTML, Anthropic posts, METR blog, Bowne-Anderson substack, Agentless GitHub, OpenAI o1 system card, Sutton essay).*

### Confirmed

- **Sutton quotes** — "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin" and the plateaus/inhibits-progress passage are verbatim in [The Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html).
- **τ-bench pass^8 < 25% in retail** — abstract of arXiv 2406.12045 confirms: "even state-of-the-art function calling agents (like gpt-4o)… are quite inconsistent (pass^8 <25% in retail)."
- **METR 7-month doubling + sequencing failure mode** — arXiv 2503.14499 / METR blog confirm "doubling approximately every seven months since 2019" and "AI agents often seem to struggle with stringing together longer sequences of actions more than they lack skills."
- **Anthropic SWE-bench minimal scaffold** — swe-bench-sonnet post confirms 49% on SWE-bench Verified with "a simple prompt and two general purpose tools" (bash + edit) and the philosophy "give as much control as possible to the language model itself, and keep the scaffolding minimal."
- **Building Effective Agents quotes** — both the "simple, composable patterns" sentence and the workflows-vs-agents definitions are verbatim in the Anthropic article.
- **arXiv 2607.03691 exists and matches** — "Don't Blame the Large Language Model: How Agent Harness Evolution Shapes Coding Agent Quality"; 35 sequential releases of the Qwen Code CLI, LLM held constant, quality traced to harness changes. As described.
- **Bowne-Anderson harness post** — the "strip away structure" quote, Manus "re-architected five times since March 2024," Open Deep Research rebuilds, and Claude Code strip-downs all appear verbatim. (Correctly rated "Medium — practitioner testimony" in the evidence table.)
- **~50× cost claim (AI Agents That Matter)** — paper HTML confirms LATS costs "over 50 times more" than the warming baseline and that costs "can differ by almost two orders of magnitude" at comparable accuracy. The doc's phrasing is a fair summary, though 50× is specifically LATS-vs-warming, not all complex agents.
- **Agentless three-stage pipeline, best open-source, lowest cost** — confirmed (localization → repair → patch validation).
- **OpenAI adopted Agentless** — confirmed in the o1 system card (arXiv 2412.16720): "we use the best-performing open-source scaffold at the time of our initial implementation, Agentless." See caveat below on attributing this to o3 specifically.

### Corrected

- **τ-bench ">60% average success"** — WRONG as stated. The abstract says agents "succeed on **<50% of the tasks**" on average. The ~61% figure is gpt-4o's *retail-domain* single-run score only; airline is far lower. Doc should read: "<50% average single-run success (≈61% in retail) but pass^8 < 25%." The rhetorical point (inconsistency) survives, but the headline number was overstated.
- **Agentless "27.33% at $0.34"** — these are the v1-paper/GitHub-README figures (27.3%, $0.34). The current arXiv abstract reports **32.00% at $0.70** on SWE-bench Lite. Either cite the version or update the numbers; "highest among open-source" holds in both versions.
- **"80%-reliability horizon roughly 5× shorter"** — METR's paper says "**4-6x shorter**" (Section 3.2.1). "~5×" is inside the range but should be quoted as 4–6× to match the source.
- **OpenAI adoption "for o3's SWE-bench numbers"** — documented adoption is for the **o1** system card; the o3 attribution rests only on an unfetchable x.com post. Safer phrasing: "adopted by OpenAI as its SWE-bench scaffold (o1 system card; reportedly also o3)."

### Could not verify (treat with caution)

- **"by 2026 frontier 50%-horizons are reported around 12h"** — no source given anywhere in the doc; not in the METR paper fetched. Remove or source it.
- **x.com/philschmid link** (o3–Agentless) — unfetchable; covered by the correction above.
- **Secondary/aggregator links not individually checked**: lowtouch.ai, Honeycomb, agentpatterns.ai, mer.vin, augmentcode.com and jonbeckett.com (the "Karpathy guidelines measured" claims rest entirely on these two unverified 2026 blog posts — weakest sourcing in the doc), theneuron.ai, starlog.is, shashankguda.medium.com, amux.io, Arize. None is load-bearing for the core argument, but the Karpathy-guidelines "measurably reduced tool calls, time, and cost" claim should be flagged as unverified practitioner claims.
- **Dwarkesh interviews (Sutton 2025, Karpathy Oct 2025)** — not fetched; the "decade of agents" framing is widely corroborated (HN thread cited), low risk.

**Overall verdict:** the document's synthesis and direction-of-evidence are sound; every pro-scaffold pillar (τ-bench inconsistency, METR reliability gap, Agentless, AI Agents That Matter, harness-evolution study) checks out in substance. Problems are numeric overstatements (τ-bench >60%, exact Agentless figures, 5× vs 4–6×), one unsourced 2026 figure (12h horizon), and one misattributed adoption detail (o3 vs o1).
